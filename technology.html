BEHIND THE SCENES / 02

A world shaped by mathematics

River Run 3D is an independent project inspired by the arcade adventures of the ZX Spectrum era. Its procedural 3D world is rendered with ray marching in a WebGPU shader.

Not affiliated with or endorsed by any classic game creator or publisher.

What is ray marching?

For each pixel, the renderer sends a ray from the camera into the scene and advances it in steps, asking how far the nearest surface is at each position — large distances allow longer steps, while nearby surfaces call for smaller ones

When the ray gets close enough to a surface, the shader shades that point using its material, surface direction, and lighting — rays that escape the scene show the sky

  1. CastStart a ray at the camera
  2. MeasureEstimate distance to the scene
  3. AdvanceMove along the ray and repeat
  4. ShadeColor the surface when reached

Many thanks to Inigo Quilez for the detailed explanation of this technology in Raymarching distance fields — a valuable guide to understanding how distance functions become rendered worlds

Shapes as distance fields

A signed distance function, or SDF, describes a shape through the distance to its surface — positive outside, zero on the surface, and negative inside

The shader combines mathematical shapes to form planes, ships, helicopters, bridges, and other objects, while the landscape follows a terrain height field and sampled river profiles

The scene combines distance estimates, so the marcher uses conservative steps and iteration limits to balance detail with rendering cost

WebGPU + WGSL

TypeScript runs the game simulation and sends camera settings, river samples, and nearby objects to GPU buffers — a full-screen triangle invokes the WGSL fragment shader that renders the view

Surface normals are estimated from changes in the scene distance, and additional rays estimate soft shadows — procedural materials add rock, grass, water highlights, and metallic surfaces

Ray marching can be expensive, especially on phones — the renderer limits work using object bounds, spatial chunks, and adaptive ray-march and shadow quality

A repeatable procedural world

A seeded generator builds river bends, islands, bridges, fuel stops, and enemy placements — the same seed and river setting reproduce the same route

As the camera moves, a window of river samples and nearby objects is uploaded to the GPU, keeping the shader focused on the current stretch of the world

Synthesized sound

The Web Audio API generates engine noise, shots, explosions, splashes, and game cues procedurally — sound is off by default and can be enabled or muted with the Sound button below the game

Running in your browser

Playing requires a browser with an available WebGPU adapter — support and performance depend on the device, browser, and graphics drivers
